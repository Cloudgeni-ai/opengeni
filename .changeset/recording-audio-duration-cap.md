Recording audio is put on its timeline in bounded memory. The timeline filter filled each hole in a track's timestamps with silence in a single step and held that whole stretch in memory, so a few-kilobyte chunk whose packets claimed a much later timestamp could make ffmpeg allocate gigabytes. Each run's packet timestamps are now read first, by a stream copy that never decodes; the run is then decoded plainly and put back on its timestamps while it streams to the encoder, with silence written in fixed-size pieces, so memory no longer depends on how long a hole is. No hole is too long: a browser track whose audio source stops or is suspended leaves a hole as long as the quiet, and such recordings are kept. A run whose timeline, or the audio its packets hold, runs more than five seconds and 1% past what its chunks declared fails as `invalid_audio` before anything is decoded, which is not retried. A file brought in without a known length, such as a WebM or Ogg file a phone cannot measure, may be as long as a recording can be (ten hours). A prepared file that still comes out materially longer than declared also fails as `invalid_audio` instead of being stored.
